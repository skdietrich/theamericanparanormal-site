History of Spiritualism in America

American Spiritualism made an extraordinary proposition: the dead could communicate with the living. Its history is also a history of how Americans argued about testimony, instruments, belief, and fraud.

The Fox sisters and the problem of a convincing demonstration

In 1848, reports of mysterious knocks associated with Kate and Maggie Fox in New York helped launch a movement. The Library of Congress places Spiritualism’s period of broad popularity between the 1840s and the 1920s. Séances and mediums brought an apparently private question—what happens after death—into public discussion. [1]

A reported knock was not just a strange sound. If it could answer a question, it seemed to offer a system of communication. That distinction matters when reading an account: did witnesses describe a noise, interpret it as an answer, or record a repeatable exchange? Those are different kinds of evidence, even when a storyteller presents them as one event.

A movement disputed in its own time

Spiritualism did not develop without opposition. The Library of Congress also preserves Reuben Briggs Davenport’s 1888 The Death-Blow to Spiritualism, an openly hostile account built around the Fox sisters’ story and testimony. [2] Reading a contemporary critic alongside an advocate reveals the struggle over who could speak authoritatively about an experience.

Neither a believer’s enthusiasm nor a critic’s title settles the historical question. Ask who wrote the account, when it was written, what the writer witnessed personally, and what interests shaped its publication. A later retelling may turn a disputed demonstration into a tidy origin story. A primary source often leaves the argument less tidy—and more revealing.

When investigation became part of the story

The Library of Congress’s teaching material contrasts William Crookes’s investigations with the skeptical Seybert Commission’s 1887 report. [1] These cases make useful starting points for examining experimental controls: who selected the conditions, who observed the demonstration, and what would have counted against the claim?

The arrival of a new instrument changes what an investigator can record. It also introduces new ways to make a mistake. A device can preserve an observation without establishing its cause. This is the thread that connects a séance account to a modern audio file: the record and the explanation must be evaluated separately.

How to read the history without losing the question

  • Separate contemporary records from later dramatic retellings.
  • Look for descriptions of conditions, rather than conclusions alone.
  • Compare advocates and critics; identify what each actually observed.
  • Keep a distinction between a movement’s historical influence and the truth of any particular claim.

That approach leaves room for curiosity while giving the reader a way to judge an argument.
